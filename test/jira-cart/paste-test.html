The Paste Test

Nine fields per issue have to land somewhere a person will read them. A table wins in Confluence and Outlook and loses everywhere people actually chat — Slack has no tables at all, and a pasted list can be reshuffled by hand where a table cannot. So: five shapes, shown in the three places the clipboard really goes. Switch shapes and fields until one of them is obviously right.

Two questions, one page, since 1.2.0. Above the fold is what a paste looks like — and Issue reference is the one instrument here that is a shipped preference, so the five line shapes can be put on a real clipboard and pasted per target, which is the table appendix A.9.1 never recorded. At the foot is the ⚙ bench, absorbed from the configurability prototype: whether the settings screen fits and reads in a drawer that can be 300×215px. The two do not drive each other, and the fence at the bottom says so.

A fourth structure is a PROPOSAL, not a shipped screen. The bench's Tabs switch gained Presets · proposed, which is the export-presets design made pressable. Set Width to 300 min, Height to 215 min and Zoom to 1×, and it is there to answer four things no argument can: whether four full tab labels fit; how many rows the foot takes with three arrows and therefore what the drawer's minimum height becomes — the readout under the drawer prints both, measured; whether the arrow is noticed at rest, which is what the Arrow switch is for; and whether the split between the preset you are editing and the preset that prints is confusing in the hand. That last one is the one to take most seriously: the last effort's panel layout was settled by argument, prototyped, chosen — and then reversed twice by somebody pressing it.

What survives a chat paste

Links, bold and bullet lists survive everywhere. They are the common denominator across all seven targets above, which is what makes a list the safe shape.

A colour is only safe if it brings its own background. Flipping the client theme to dark found this: the status lozenge sets both background and text, so it survives either ground as a self-contained sticker — the fix-version chip set only text, and its fixed grey went unreadable.

Then Outlook killed the elegant fix. Pasted with keep source formatting on 2026-08-20, Outlook stripped every opacity and every inline border — it renders HTML through Word's engine. So the muting is one fixed grey instead, and the fix versions are joined by a comma rather than divided by boxes that turned out not to exist.

And Teams does its own theming. The same grey span came out dark in the light skin and light in the dark skin — Teams re-maps text colour to whatever skin it is in. So the grey chosen for Outlook costs nothing there, and the light-versus-dark problem simply does not exist in Teams.

That last row is the counterintuitive one. Making the pill bolder so it would survive is exactly the wrong move: Teams keeps a pale ground and discards a saturated one, then re-maps the white text to its own skin, so a bold pill loses its ground and its colour and comes back as bold black. The fix for an invisible pill is a slightly stronger tint, not a solid fill.

Two trailing spaces are markdown's hard line break. The two-line shape needs them, they are invisible, and some editors strip them on save. They are marked in the plain-text panel so you can see what you are relying on.

Reshuffling it afterwards

The paste is not the end. People reorder the list in the editor they pasted it into — to put the urgent thing first, or to group what they are talking about. How easily a shape can be reshuffled by hand is a real criterion, and it is the second argument against the table.

It also rules out anything positional. Printing the epic's name only on its first appearance would kill the repetition neatly — and would then be wrong the moment somebody moved a line, with nothing on the page to say so. A field either says the same thing on every row or it cannot be trusted after a reshuffle.

Deliberately absent — do not read as an omission

  • The two-step button. No fetch, no 📋 Table → Copy table 12 ladder, no ⚠️. That behaviour is already settled; this page is only about the shape of what comes out.
  • The Cart's REAL behaviour. There is a drawer on this page — the ⚙ bench below is one, at the real 300×215 — and this line used to say there was not. It was true when the page held only the paste rack and it has been false since the bench landed at 1.2.0. Corrected 2026-08-27. What is absent is everything the drawer does: no fetch, no 📋 Details → Copy ladder, no ⚠️, no clipboard behind the six foot buttons, no store, and no second tab writing.
  • ch is measured with the wrong ruler. The two stepped buttons reserve min-inline-size: 11ch, and a ch is the width of a 0 in the inherited font family. This page inherits IBM Plex Sans; the drawer inherits Jira's own stack. The foot's four other values were made byte-identical to the script on 2026-08-27 — after a drift there invalidated a measurement — but this one cannot be, so the arrows-versus-rows answer is close rather than exact.
  • Everything the Presets · proposed variant models is a PROPOSAL. It is the only variant that adds controls the shipped Cart does not have, which breaks this page's own rule that the variants differ only in arrangement — said out loud beside TAB_SETS rather than left to be discovered. Its presets live in a variable and nowhere else: no gt-jira-cart.presets key, no normalisation, no repair of a hand-edited entry, and nothing survives a reload.
  • What an arrow pick would do. The list opens and the pick sticks in the dropdown, and that is all. The rule it is standing in for — the arrow does what its button does — needs a fetch and a clipboard, and neither is on this page.
  • Teams' and Slack's real renderers. The chat panel is a width and a typeface, not a simulation. It is honest about the 430px constraint and about nothing else — which is exactly why the Copy this shape for real button is there.
  • A markdown renderer. Nothing on this page turns markdown into HTML. The two rich panels show what the text/html flavour emits; the mono panel shows the text/plain bytes. They are two independent outputs, not one converted twice.
  • Field order. The order fields appear in the meta line is a proposal and nothing more. So is which fields default to on.
  • Reordering the collection. Not here, and not proposed. The reshuffling this page cares about happens after the paste, in Teams or Outlook or a text file, by hand. The Cart still emits the collection in insertion order and still has no reorder control.
  • The ⚙ bench drives nothing above it. Its field lists, its bands and its own Issue reference dropdown set the bench's state and no specimen re-renders. They are two instruments answering two questions — what a paste looks like, and whether the settings fit — and joining them would make each one worse at its own job. The rack's Issue reference is the one that reaches the clipboard.
  • The bench's --cart-* colours are an APPROXIMATION of the Cart's own chrome, not a slice of the script's stylesheet. Close enough to judge a layout by, and not evidence about a colour — for that, read injectStyle in the script, and css-smoke.mjs for what is asserted about it.
  • The bench remembers its tab in localStorage, and the script does not. In the script it is one string in gt-jira-cart.prefs, range-checked in normalisePrefs exactly as layout and corner are. It is localStorage here so you can watch it survive a reload without installing anything. Nothing else on the bench is stored.
  • The bench's drawer body is a sketch. Seven rows and two chips, there only so you can see what ⚙ replaces. No fetch, no arming ladder, no 📋 Fetching…, no expiry, no per-button held result. The foot's six buttons do nothing.
  • Two of the bench's three tab structures were rejected. Three tabs shipped; two and four are §4's rows, kept so the choice can be looked at rather than read about. All three are built from the same four groups, so no control exists in one and not another.
  • Five of the six issues are real, read from dalet.atlassian.net on 2026-08-20 — two types, three status categories, two priorities, a missing assignee, a missing fix version, one issue carrying two fix versions, and two with no parent. GLX-402 is invented, and stands for an issue Jira returns nothing about.
